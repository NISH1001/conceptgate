Operators of deployed language models must detect and control concepts such as a jailbreak attempt, a topic, or a tone. Often they have few examples and cannot retrain the model. We study one residual-stream direction, fit in closed form from about ten examples per class, that both reads a concept and steers generation. We test it on five models up to 2.6B parameters against a logistic probe on the same layers, matched-norm random directions, and random selection. We report three findings. First, steering along the few-shot direction moves refusal both up and down. On Qwen2.5-0.5B, refusal is 64% unsteered, 35% when we steer away, and 79% when we steer toward. Second, the direction reads the surface form of an attack, not its intent. On two models and on 1,725 real prompts, a gate on the direction fires on 93% of harmless commands to the model and on 53% of attacks written as ordinary requests. Its false-positive rate changes from 18% to 96% with the register of benign traffic. Third, steerability is predictable per prompt. On Qwen2.5-0.5B, the unsteered activations predict how far a fixed write moves a prompt's sampled refusal rate (Spearman rho = 0.58, permutation z = 6.7). As a steering trigger, this predictor beats random selection at every tested coverage. The concept gate reads the same quantity with the wrong sign (rho = -0.30). On gemma-2-2b the write barely moves refusal, so this claim is untested there. The controls also show where the design adds nothing: the detector matches the logistic probe, and depth fusion and mixture densities give no gain on real models. Two lessons for steering evaluations follow. A missing chat template reversed the sign of an early result, and an unvalidated outcome measure gave a false null.
