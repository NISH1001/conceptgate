Operators of deployed language models must detect and control concepts such as a jailbreak attempt, a topic, or a tone. Often they have few examples and cannot retrain the model. One residual-stream direction can detect a concept, and the same direction, added back, can steer generation. We test how far this goes with about ten examples per class and closed-form fitting. ConceptGate reads a concept at several layers, fuses the layers with a learned depth filter, gates on a calibrated likelihood ratio, and steers with a direction fit from the same examples. Against fair baselines on five models up to 2.6B parameters, most of the design fails. A logistic probe on the same layers matches the detector. Depth fusion and mixture densities add nothing on real models. A gate on concept presence selects nothing, because it fires on 97% of formatted attacks. One early result favoured the gate. It came from a missing chat template. Three results survive. First, the few-shot direction is a refusal lever in both directions (Qwen2.5-0.5B refusal: 64% unsteered, 35% away, 79% toward). Second, the direction reads the surface form of an attack, not its intent, on two models and on real prompts. The gate fires on 93% of harmless commands to the model, 99% of harmless roleplay prompts, and 53% of attacks written as ordinary requests. Its false-positive rate changes from 18% to 96% with the register of benign traffic. Third, on Qwen2.5-0.5B, the unsteered activations predict how far a fixed write moves a prompt's sampled refusal rate (Spearman rho = 0.58, permutation z = 6.7). The concept gate reads this steerability at rho = -0.30, with the wrong sign. As a trigger, predicted steerability beats random selection at every tested coverage. On gemma-2-2b, the write barely moves refusal, so the claim is untested there. We also show how an unvalidated outcome measure gave a false null.
